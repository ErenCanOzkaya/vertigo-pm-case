From Five Ideas to a Real Experiment

My original Q3 listed five test ideas. Re-reading it, I realized I had written five ideas — not five experiments. No prioritization, no test design, and one variant that contradicted my own stance on manipulation. This page is the deepened version: how I'd sequence the five, one of them designed end to end, and the contradiction fixed.

Section One

Why I Revisited My Own Answer

Three gaps I only saw clearly after submission.

Gap 01

Ideas, not experiments.

I named target KPIs, but listing metrics isn't designing a test. There was no sample sizing, no duration, no guardrails, no kill conditions — and no acknowledgement that the metric I most cared about, LTV, can't be read quickly. A test idea becomes a PM's work only when it has a stopping rule and a decision rule.

Gap 02

No prioritization.

Five tests, but several touch the same surface and can't run on the same players at once. I never said which ships first or why. Prioritizing under interaction constraints is the actual job.

Gap 03

An internal contradiction.

Test 1's "expiring starter kit" loss-aversion mechanic is a dark pattern — and elsewhere I argued mid-core players are the most sensitive to exactly that. My own variant violated my own diagnosis.

Section Two

Prioritization — Sequencing Five Interacting Tests

Five tests can't run simultaneously, because three of them touch perceived fairness (bots, auto-aim, spend matchmaking) and two touch the IAP surface (IAP overhaul, home screen) — run together, they contaminate each other's read. I sequenced them on three criteria.

Criterion 01

Read speed

D1 metrics read in days; D30 and LTV in weeks. Fast-reading tests go first, so the org learns sooner and de-risks the slower ones.

Criterion 02

Revenue risk

Does it touch the revenue core? Low-risk, cheap-to-learn tests run first; revenue-critical ones wait until they can be armed with an early signal.

Criterion 03

Contamination

Tests on the same surface must be separated in time. Three touch perceived fairness; two touch the IAP surface — run together they'd corrupt each other's read.

The roadmap — sequenced, not ranked by excitement

Phase 1 · Parallel
Home Screen + Bot Composition
Distinct surfaces (menu UI vs in-match), low revenue risk, fast read. Home Screen also gives a cheap early signal on whether reducing IAP pressure moves retention — de-risking Phase 2.
›
Phase 2 · Headline
IAP Overhaul
The headline test, but not first: revenue-critical with a long observation window. Run it armed with the Phase 1 signal and pre-registered kill metrics. Designed in full below.
›
Phase 3 · Precondition
Matchmaking by Spend Tier
Held back as a design precondition, not for uncertainty: it needs a whale counter-design and an ethics/comms decision before any A/B, since it reads as manipulative if discovered.
›
Conditional · Last
Auto-Aim Reduction
The highest-risk test — it nerfs the most-engaged players. Only run it if Phase 1–2 show fairness perception actually moves retention, then on a tiny cohort with tier-level churn as the kill metric.

Impact vs Risk — the whole portfolio at a glance

Impact = potential retention / LTV lift  ·  Risk = revenue + execution + perception. Hover or tap a test.

Five tests, one portfolio

Top-left is the quick-win zone (low risk, high impact); top-right needs careful design (high risk, high impact). IAP Overhaul — ringed — is the one specced end to end below.

Section Three

Deep Dive — IAP Overhaul as a Real Experiment

This is the test I'd take all the way — both because it's the headline and because it forces the hardest experimentation problem: a metric you can't read quickly.

Hypothesis
Reducing offer frequency and improving timing will lower short-term (D0) conversion volume but raise D30 retention and cohort LTV — by letting attachment form before monetizing, targeting moments of genuine intent, and preserving the effectiveness of future offers instead of training players to dismiss everything on sight.
Who's in the test
New-install cohort, randomized at install. This runs on new users only — the staging is about first sessions, and existing users have already formed their habits. Testing it on them would measure the wrong thing.
Variants
A · Control

Current behavior — 5–10 offers at session start.

B · Treatment

Three stages: no offers in the first 3 sessions; one value-framed, relevant offer after session 3; then low-frequency, behavior-triggered offers at peak-engagement moments, such as just after a strong match.

A later arm could isolate frequency from timing, but adding it now would dilute statistical power — I'd treat it as a follow-up rather than diluting the read here.

Primary metric
Cohort LTV at D30. Because LTV lags, the in-test decision drivers are D30 retention plus the early ARPU/ARPPU trajectory, used as leading indicators.
Guardrail metrics
Cohort D0 and D7 revenue (to catch a revenue collapse early), ARPDAU, conversion rate (to confirm the expected short-term dip stays within tolerance), and D1 retention / uninstall rate — the retention side must actually move; if it doesn't, the thesis is failing.
Kill conditions Pre-registered
Stop if treatment D0+D7 revenue per cohort drops more than ~25–30% and D1/D7 retention shows no statistically meaningful lift by day 14 — revenue pain with no retention payoff means the thesis is dead. Plus any catastrophic guardrail breach (crash spike, uninstall spike). Pre-registering these stops me from rationalizing a failing test mid-flight.
Sample & duration
Powered for the primary metric — baseline retention/ARPU, a chosen minimum detectable effect, 80% power, 95% significance. The test runs several weeks (≈5–6) so D30 can be observed on a full cohort, not days. Interim monitoring is on guardrails only; I never peek at the primary metric, to avoid false positives from repeated looks.
The hard part — LTV lag
You cannot read 90-day LTV in a 6-week test. So the decision is made on leading indicators (D30 retention + ARPU trajectory), while a longer holdout is kept on each variant to confirm true LTV over time. The ship decision is a bet on leading indicators that the holdout later validates or corrects — and being explicit about that is the honest version of an LTV-based test.

Decision matrix — the rule is written before the data arrives

Outcome Call Why
Retention ↑  +  revenue flat or ↑ Ship Clear win — better retention at no revenue cost.
Retention ↑  +  revenue ↓ short-term, projected LTV ↑ Ship This is the thesis. The trade is intentional, and the leading indicators plus holdout justify it.
Retention flat  +  revenue ↓ Kill Paid revenue for nothing — no retention payoff to bank on.
Retention ↓ Kill regardless The premise failed; nothing downstream rescues it.
Section Four

Fixing the Contradiction in Test 1

My original Test 1 included a loss-aversion variant: give new players a free starter kit, let it expire after a few sessions, then present the first offer as a way to "keep what you have." That is a dark pattern — it manufactures a loss to sell the cure. And I'd argued elsewhere that mid-core players are the most sensitive audience to exactly this.

✗ Before — loss-framed (dark pattern)

A free starter kit is given, then expires after a few sessions. The first offer is framed as a way to "keep what you have" — manufacturing a loss the player never actually chose, then selling the cure. It works on reflex, not on value, and it's exactly the manipulation a mid-core audience punishes in reviews.

✓ After — value-framed (forward value)

After three unmonetized sessions, the single first offer is framed as forward value: tied to what the player is already progressing toward, shown with an honest shop-value comparison, no countdown pressure, no revoked gift. The test stays about offer timing; the manipulation is gone.

Loss-framing becomes value-framing. This also makes Test 1 consistent with Piggy Bank 2.0 — sell speed, not the ceiling, and keep nothing hidden.

Section Five

Open Risks & What I'd Watch

The experiment design isn't bulletproof. These are the things most likely to fool the read — and how I'd guard against each.

⚠ Novelty effects

A new IAP rhythm can lift engagement simply because it's different, not because it's better. The longer holdout helps separate a novelty bump from a durable effect before anything ships permanently.

⚠ Cohort seasonality

A cohort acquired during an event or a UA spike isn't representative of normal intake. I'd avoid launching the test across an atypical acquisition window that would bias the cohort.

⚠ The temptation to peek

Reading the primary metric repeatedly inflates false positives. Hence guardrail-only interim checks and a pre-registered decision date — the discipline is the safeguard, not good intentions.

⚠ Generalizability

A result on new users doesn't automatically apply to existing players. The staged approach is built for first sessions, so I'd be careful not to over-claim it as a whole-population fix.
