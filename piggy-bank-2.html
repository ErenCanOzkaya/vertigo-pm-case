Section One

Why I Revisited My Own Design

My original Piggy Bank contained two contradictions I only saw clearly after submission.

Flaw 01

I designed the thing I spent the whole case criticizing.

The original bank accumulated "exclusive permanent gear that cannot be purchased directly from the store," with payment as the fastest route to it. Throughout Q1–Q3, I argued that Critical Strike's biggest churn driver is pay-to-win resentment: the feeling that money, not skill, decides outcomes. Locking exclusive competitive gear behind a paid bank is precisely that mechanic. My own feature violated my own diagnosis.

Flaw 02

Approach A killed my own KPI.

I proposed a battle-pass-integrated bank claimable only by premium pass holders, while naming "first-time IAP conversions among previously non-spending players" as a key KPI. A bank only payers can open converts no one. The two halves of my answer were working against each other.

The redesign below fixes both — and in fixing them, it became a more honest and, I believe, more commercially effective feature.

Section Two

Design Principles

Four rules govern every decision in the new design.

Rule 01

Sell speed, never the ceiling.

The bank never contains power a player couldn't earn through normal play. It only shortens the road. Payment buys time, not advantage.

Rule 02

The ignorability test.

A player who completely ignores the bank must lose nothing permanent. If ignoring a monetization feature makes you permanently weaker, the feature is soft-mandatory — and that is where pay-to-win perception begins.

Rule 03

Every rule fits in one sentence.

If a rule can't be explained to the player in a single sentence, it reads as manipulation — especially to mid-core players, who are the most sensitive audience to dark patterns. No hidden multipliers, no fake timers, no expiring gifts.

Rule 04

Two segments, two banks.

Never-paid players get the conversion bank described below. Existing payers get a battle-pass-integrated vault (my original Approach A, now correctly positioned as a payer retention tool rather than a conversion tool). Each segment gets the version built for it, and each version gets its own KPI.

Section Three

How It Works

A six-step loop — fills through play, proves value before any purchase

Step 1
Organic discovery
After your 5th completed match, a small bank icon appears in the HUD showing fill progress. No popup, no interruption — you tap it when curiosity wins.
›
Step 2
Fills through play
Early levels fill on matches, kills and wins; higher levels shift to skill actions — headshots, grenade kills, MVP. Each contributing action flies a coin into the bank.
›
Step 3
Your own next goal
The bank targets the next item on your existing ladder. "This bank contains your MCX — shop value 5,000 cash." Nothing inside is bank-exclusive power; only cosmetics are exclusive.
›
Step 4
Full bank unlocks a trial
When the bank is full, you get a clearly labeled 5-match trial of the reward. You feel the difference before any purchase decision exists. No blind buying, no buyer's remorse.
›
Step 5
Three honest paths
Open now at 25–30% below shop value, keep playing to unlock it free at 3–4× the threshold, or pass — carrying 30% of the progress you filled into the next bank.
›
Step 6
The bank levels up
Each new bank targets the next, more valuable item. Fill requirement, reward value and price all grow — but the discount ratio never changes. Every level offers the same deal.

Section Four

Why This Works in a Mid-Core FPS

First purchases fail for three reasons, and each mechanic above answers one of them.

The identity barrier

"I don't pay for mobile games" is a self-image, not a budget constraint. The first purchase is an identity shift, not a microtransaction. The bank reframes the purchase so that self-image survives it: the player isn't buying power — they're unlocking something they already earned through play, a few days early. The free path makes this structurally true, not just marketing language: everything in the bank is reachable without paying.

Value uncertainty

A hesitant player can't judge what $1.99 is worth in a game they suspect of squeezing them. The trial eliminates the judgment problem entirely — they've already used the item for five matches. The shop-value comparison makes the bargain verifiable: the player can open the store and check the math themselves. Trust through verifiability, not through promises.

Why mid-core specifically

Piggy banks traditionally fail in mid-core for the three reasons in my original Q4: currency complexity, competitive scrutiny, manipulation sensitivity. This design turns each weakness into the feature's foundation. Currency complexity disappears because the bank contains one concrete item the player already wants, not an abstract currency pile. Competitive scrutiny is satisfied because the reward rule guarantees zero new power sources — the game's power curve is untouched; the bank only moves a player a few days along a curve everyone travels. And manipulation sensitivity is answered by the one-sentence rule and the trial: a mid-core player can audit every part of this system and find nothing hidden.

In a competitive FPS, fairness isn't a tone of voice — it's whether the guy who killed you has something you can never have. Here, he doesn't.

Why the reward must come from the existing ladder

This is the load-bearing rule. If the bank contained bank-exclusive power — even fully earnable free — every death to that item would be attributed to the feature by name, the balance team would inherit a permanent new liability, and the free path would force every player through a monetization surface to access power. Drawing rewards from the existing ladder means the bank changes when players get things, never what exists. The difference between selling a shortcut on an existing road and building a toll road.

Why this builds a paying relationship, not just a payment

The leveled structure means the first purchase is not an endpoint. The player who converts at Level 1 meets the identical, transparent deal at Level 2. Habit is not engineered through escalating pressure — it emerges from a repeated positive experience: I played, I earned, I verified the value, I paid, I was not tricked. That is the only kind of payment habit that survives contact with a mid-core community.

Section Five

KPIs & Guardrails

Metrics — What Success Looks Like

Primary KPI

First-purchase conversion rate among never-paid players exposed to the bank.

Supporting — Repeat Rate

Repeat purchase rate within 30 days of first purchase — tests the habit thesis, not just the conversion.

Supporting — Cohort Retention

Retention delta of converted cohort vs matched non-converted cohort. Are converts staying, or buying and leaving?

Supporting — Free-Path Completion

Free-path completion rate — is the free path real, or theoretical?

Guardrails — What Must Never Happen

Manipulation Sentiment

Pay-to-win and manipulation mention rate in store reviews. The bank must never appear as a named villain.

Win-Rate Divergence

Within-league win-rate divergence by spend tier. The acceleration scale must stay below what a same-league opponent can feel in a match.

The Underlying Bet

Slower, trust-based conversion beats fast, resentment-based conversion on 30-day value — because the first payment habit only survives if the player was never tricked.

Section Six

Open Risks & How I Would Test Them

I'd rather name these myself than have them found for me.

⚠ Trial-end risk

Taking the reward back after 5 matches structurally resembles the "expiring gift" pattern I rejected in Test 1. The difference is contract vs deception: a trial declares its boundary up front; an expiring gift is framed as ownership and then revoked. Still, the emotional risk is real — I would A/B the trial length and monitor sentiment in post-trial behavior (rage-quit rate, session drop after trial end).

⚠ Calibration risk

The free path must be genuinely reachable or the whole fairness claim collapses into an anchor trick. I would calibrate thresholds from median play-rate data per level (targeting predictable play-days, not raw event counts) and A/B the paid-to-free ratio (3× vs 4×).

⚠ Discount-band perception

Even with a constant discount ratio, rising absolute prices can read as escalation. The bank screen should always show the math: shop value, bank price, discount — same three numbers at every level.

⚠ Cannibalization check

The bank must convert non-payers, not reroute existing shop spenders into a discount channel. Holdout comparison of shop revenue between bank-exposed and control cohorts.

Section Seven

What Changed

  Original design Piggy Bank 2.0
Reward Bank-exclusive permanent gear, unavailable in store Always the next item on the player's existing ladder; exclusive content is cosmetic only
Who can open Approach A: premium pass holders only Conversion bank for never-paid players; separate pass-integrated vault for payers
Value proof "Discover it organically" 5-match trial before any purchase decision
Fairness Implied Structural: free path + ignorability test + zero new power sources
Loss aversion Expiring starter kit (dark pattern) Full-bank overflow + transparent carry rule — no deception
Pricing Three fixed tiers ($1.99 / $2.99 / $4.99) Constant discount ratio (~25–30% below shop value) across leveling banks
KPI First-time conversion (contradicted by design) First conversion + repeat rate + converted-cohort retention + guardrails

The redesign didn't add features — it removed contradictions. Every change traces back to a single question I should have asked the first time: would a mid-core player who audits this feature line by line still feel respected? Piggy Bank 2.0 is the version that survives that audit.
